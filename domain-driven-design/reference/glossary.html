Glossary

The canonical vocabulary for this course, following Khononov's Learning Domain-Driven Design. Lessons use these terms and no others.

Part I Strategic design

Business domain
The company's main area of activity: the service it provides to its customers. Ch. 1
Subdomain
A fine-grained area of business activity. Subdomains are discovered from the business strategy, not designed. Ch. 1
Core subdomain
What the company does differently from its competitors: its competitive advantage. Usually complex and volatile. Build it in-house. Ch. 1
Generic subdomain
A complex but already-solved problem that every company handles the same way (identity, payments). Buy or adopt an existing solution. Ch. 1
Supporting subdomain
Needed by the business but gives no competitive advantage. Typically simple (often CRUD or ETL). Build it in-house cheaply or outsource it. Ch. 1
Ubiquitous language
The single language shared by domain experts and engineers, used in conversations, documents, and code. Each term has exactly one meaning. Avoid: technical jargon in its place. Ch. 2
Bounded context
The explicit boundary within which a model and its ubiquitous language are consistent. It's a design decision, it's owned by exactly one team, and it is also a physical boundary (it can be deployed on its own). Ch. 3
Context map
A diagram of the bounded contexts and the integration patterns between them. Ch. 4
Partnership
Cooperation pattern: two teams coordinate integration ad hoc and succeed or fail together. Ch. 4
Shared kernel
Cooperation pattern: a small part of the model is shared and co-owned by both teams. Changes to it need both teams to agree. Ch. 4
Conformist
Customer–supplier pattern: the downstream context accepts the upstream model as it is. Ch. 4
Anticorruption layer (ACL)
Customer–supplier pattern: the downstream context translates the upstream model into its own, protecting it from upstream changes. Typical when the downstream context is core. Ch. 4
Open-host service (OHS)
Customer–supplier pattern: the upstream context exposes a public published language that is separate from its internal model, so the two can evolve independently. Ch. 4
Separate ways
The teams don't integrate at all. Each context duplicates whatever functionality it needs. Ch. 4

Part II Tactical design

Transaction script
Business logic organized as procedures, each wrapping one operation in a transaction. Ch. 5
Active record
Objects that wrap data structures and carry their own persistence (CRUD) methods. Suits simple logic over complex data. Ch. 5
Domain model
Business logic expressed as objects (value objects, entities, aggregates, domain services) using the ubiquitous language. Suits complex logic. Ch. 6
Value object
An immutable object identified by the combination of its values. It has no ID. Ch. 6
Entity
An object with an identity that stays the same as its state changes. Ch. 6
Aggregate
A cluster of entities and value objects that forms a consistency and transactional boundary. It is changed only through its root's public interface. Other aggregates reference it by ID. Keep it as small as strong consistency allows. Ch. 6
Domain event
A message describing a significant business occurrence, named in the past tense (OrderPlaced). It's part of an aggregate's public interface. Ch. 6
Domain service
A stateless object holding business logic that doesn't belong to any single aggregate or value object. Ch. 6
Event-sourced domain model
A domain model whose aggregates persist state as an append-only stream of domain events. The current state is derived by replaying them. Ch. 7
Layered architecture
Presentation → business logic → data access, with an optional service layer between presentation and business logic. Ch. 8
Ports & adapters
Business logic at the center with no infrastructure dependencies. Infrastructure plugs in through ports (interfaces) and adapters (implementations). Alias: hexagonal. Ch. 8
CQRS
Separates the model that executes commands from read models (projections) optimized for queries. Ch. 8
Outbox
Commit domain events to an outbox table in the same transaction as the aggregate, then publish them reliably from there. Ch. 9
Saga
A long-running business process that reacts to events and issues commands across aggregates or contexts, with compensating actions when a step fails. Ch. 9
Process manager
A central, stateful coordinator for a multi-step process whose flow can't be derived from events alone. Ch. 9

Part III DDD in practice

Heuristic
A rule of thumb that is right most of the time, not always. Ch. 10 turns the patterns above into a decision tree. See the cheat sheet. Ch. 10
EventStorming
A workshop where people from business and engineering model a business process on a wall of sticky notes: events, commands, policies, read models, external systems, aggregates, bounded contexts. Ch. 12
Policy (EventStorming)
An automation rule: "whenever event, then command." Ch. 12
Strangler fig
Gradually replace a legacy system by routing more and more functionality to new components until the old system can be retired. Ch. 13

Part IV Relationships to other methods

Deep service
A service whose simple public interface hides a lot of functionality. A shallow service's interface is about as complex as its implementation. Ch. 14 (after Ousterhout)
Event notification
A thin event announcing that something changed. Consumers query the producer for details. Ch. 15
Event-carried state transfer (ECST)
An event that carries a snapshot of the changed state, so consumers can keep a local cache. Comes with eventual consistency. Ch. 15
Data mesh
Analytical data decentralized along domain lines. Four principles: domain ownership, data as a product, self-serve platform, federated computational governance. Ch. 16

Sources: Khononov, Learning Domain-Driven Design (O'Reilly, 2021) · Evans, DDD Reference · DDD Crew · Fowler on event-driven · Dehghani on data mesh.
