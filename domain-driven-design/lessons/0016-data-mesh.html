Data mesh

DDD's decomposition, applied to analytical data: the bounded contexts that produce the data also own and serve it.

1 · Recall first

Without looking: (a) How do analytical data models differ from operational ones? (b) What are the main weaknesses of data warehouses and data lakes? (c) Name the four principles of data mesh.

(a) Operational (OLTP) models serve real-time transactions on business entities. Analytical (OLAP) models serve insights over large volumes of history. They're structured as fact tables (business activities) and dimension tables (descriptive attributes), typically in a star or snowflake schema.
(b) Warehouse: ETL scripts pull from operational databases into one enterprise-wide model. That model is as unrealistic as one company-wide ubiquitous language, and the ETL is coupled to operational implementation details. Lake: raw data is dumped in and transformed later (ELT). It's still coupled to implementation details and risks becoming a data swamp.
(c) Domain-oriented ownership, data as a product, self-serve data platform, federated computational governance.

  • OLTP vs OLAP, plus facts and dimensions
  • Warehouse: enterprise-wide model + coupling to operational internals
  • Lake: still coupled, and the data-swamp risk
  • All four data mesh principles

2 · The ideas that matter

Two kinds of model. Operational systems model business entities for real-time transactions. Analytical systems model facts (things that happened, like business activities and events) and dimensions (the attributes you slice facts by), arranged in star or snowflake schemas. They answer different questions, so they need different models. Ch. 16.

Why the classic platforms struggle. A data warehouse extracts from operational databases into one enterprise-wide analytical model. Like a single company-wide ubiquitous language, that model rarely works, and the ETL breaks whenever an operational schema changes. A data lake stores raw data and transforms it later. It's more flexible, but it's still coupled to operational internals and tends to turn into a swamp.

Data mesh = DDD for analytics. Its four principles:

  • Domain-oriented ownership: analytical data is decomposed along the same lines as operational systems (bounded contexts), and each context's team owns its analytical data.
  • Data as a product: analytical data is served through well-defined, documented, discoverable interfaces, with real quality guarantees, much like an open-host service for analytics.
  • Self-serve data platform: a platform team gives domain teams the tooling to build and publish data products.
  • Federated computational governance: shared standards (interoperability, security, compliance) are agreed across teams and enforced by the platform automatically.

DDD tools that fit. A bounded context can produce its analytical model as a projection (the CQRS idea), and an event-sourced context can derive it straight from its events. The analytical interface is a public contract, separate from the operational internals.

See also Dehghani, "Data Mesh Principles and Logical Architecture", the origin of the four principles.

3 · Practice

In an analytical model, a table recording every completed delivery, with timestamps and amounts, is a…

  1. Dimension table
  2. Fact table
  3. Lookup table
  4. Snapshot table
Fact tables record business activities that happened. Dimension tables describe them: customer, region, courier type, and so on.

Engineers rename a column in the Orders database, and that night the warehouse's ETL silently breaks. Which weakness does this show?

  1. Analytical models cannot represent order data at all
  2. Data warehouses only work with event-sourced operational systems
  3. Star schemas cannot handle renamed columns by design
  4. ETL couples analytics to the operational implementation details
The ETL reads implementation details (operational schemas) instead of a published contract. Data mesh fixes this by having the owning team serve analytical data through an explicit interface.

Why does a single enterprise-wide warehouse model run into the same problem DDD describes in ch. 3?

  1. One model cannot fit every context's meaning consistently
  2. Analytical models must always mirror one operational schema
  3. Enterprise models require a shared kernel between teams
  4. Warehouses cannot store data from multiple bounded contexts
It's the "one ubiquitous language for the whole company" mistake again. "Customer" or "lead" means different things in different contexts, so a single model ends up serving nobody well.

The Billing team publishes a documented, versioned, discoverable "monthly revenue" dataset with quality guarantees. Which data mesh principle is this most directly?

  1. Federated computational governance
  2. Self-serve data platform
  3. Data as product
  4. Centralized data ownership
Data as a product: analytical data with real consumers, a clear interface, and quality guarantees. "Centralized ownership" is the opposite of data mesh.

All data products must use the same customer-ID format and pass automated PII checks. Teams agreed the rule together, and the platform enforces it. Which principle is this?

  1. Domain ownership
  2. Federated governance
  3. Product thinking
  4. Central warehouse
Federated computational governance: global standards decided jointly by the domain teams and enforced automatically (computationally) by the platform.

How can an event-sourced bounded context produce its analytical data product with DDD tools?

  1. Project its events into an analytical read model
  2. Let analysts query its event store tables directly
  3. Copy its operational database nightly into the lake
  4. Share its aggregates with the analytics team's codebase
A projection (the CQRS idea) builds a purpose-made analytical model from the events, behind a published interface. The other options leak implementation details again.

What does a data lake typically do differently from a data warehouse?

  1. Enforces one enterprise-wide model before any data lands
  2. Removes all coupling to operational system implementation details
  3. Assigns analytical data ownership to each producing team
  4. Stores raw data first, transforming it later on
A lake ingests raw data and transforms it later (ELT, not ETL). That's more flexible, but it doesn't remove the coupling to operational internals, and without discipline it becomes a data swamp. Per-team ownership is data mesh, not a lake.

4 · Interview scenario

FitLoop, a gym chain with an app. It has bounded contexts for Memberships, Class Booking, Personal Training (its core: adaptive training plans), and Payments (wrapping a provider). A central data team of four people maintains a warehouse fed by nightly ETL from every operational database. They're three months behind on requests, the ETL breaks often, and nobody agrees on what an "active member" is. The VP of Data asks: "Should we move to a data mesh? What would it look like here?"

(7 min) Diagnose today's problems in the chapter's terms. Then sketch a data mesh: who owns which data products, how they're produced, what the platform team does, and how governance works. Name one risk or cost.

Diagnosis: the central team is a bottleneck. The ETL is coupled to operational implementation details, so it breaks. And "active member" has several meanings, the ch. 3 problem: one enterprise-wide model can't hold every context's meaning.

Context / teamExample data product
MembershipsMembership facts (joins, cancellations, freezes), plus the official definition of an "active membership"
Class BookingBookings and attendance facts, class dimensions
Personal TrainingPlan adherence and progress facts (core, so high value)
PaymentsRevenue facts

Produced by: each team projects its analytical model from its operational model or events, and serves it through a documented, versioned interface (data as a product).

Platform: the former central data team becomes the self-serve platform team: storage, pipelines, a catalog, and access control.

Governance: federated computational. Shared member-ID and date formats and PII rules, agreed across teams and enforced by the platform.

Risk: every domain team needs data skills and capacity. With only four contexts, it may be worth starting with the most painful products before going all in.

  • Assigned data products to the owning bounded-context teams
  • Produced data via projections behind a published interface
  • Repurposed the central team as the self-serve platform
