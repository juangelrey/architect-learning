Chapter 2

Discovering domain knowledge

Software fails less because of bad code than because of misunderstood business. The fix is a language.

≈ 12 minutes 2 review + 7 questions + 1 scenario Part I

0 · Spaced review: ch. 1

A payroll startup wins on an easy-to-use interface for small businesses. Its tax calculation follows the same official rules every competitor uses, and good vendors already exist for it. Tax calculation is a…

  1. Core subdomain
  2. Generic subdomain
  3. Supporting subdomain
  4. Bounded context
Complex, but everyone does it the same way and it's solved, so it's generic: buy or adopt. The edge (core) is the user experience. Ch. 1.

What's the chapter 1 solution strategy for a supporting subdomain?

  1. Build cheaply in-house or outsource
  2. Buy an existing off-the-shelf product
  3. Assign the strongest senior engineers
  4. Use an event-sourced domain model
It's simple and gives no edge, so build it cheaply in-house or outsource it. Buying is for generic subdomains. Your strongest engineers belong on the core. Ch. 1.

1 · Recall first

Without looking: (a) What two properties must a ubiquitous language have to be consistent? (b) Why does the "telephone game" happen in software projects? (c) What is a model?

(a) No ambiguous terms (one word, one meaning) and no synonyms (one meaning, one word).
(b) Knowledge passes from domain experts to analysts, then into requirement documents, then to engineers, then into code. Some of it is lost or distorted at every hand-off.
(c) A simplified representation of something that deliberately emphasizes some aspects and ignores others, in order to solve a specific problem. It's not a copy of reality.

  • Named both "no ambiguity" and "no synonyms"
  • Explained knowledge loss across translations
  • Defined a model as purposeful simplification

2 · The ideas that matter

The problem. Business knowledge usually reaches the code second- or third-hand. Domain experts explain things to analysts, analysts write requirements, and engineers translate those into code. Each translation loses something, and the software ends up solving the wrong problem. Ch. 2.

The fix: a ubiquitous language. Engineers and domain experts speak one language: the business's own, not technical jargon. It's used everywhere: in conversations, documents, tests, and the code itself. To stay consistent it allows no ambiguous terms and no synonyms.

It's a model. A model is useful because of what it leaves out. Think of a subway map: it's wrong about distances and right for its purpose. The ubiquitous language models the business: its entities, its behavior, its rules. It isn't meant to mirror the whole world.

Cultivating it is continuous work. Keep a glossary (a wiki works) for the nouns. Capture behavior and rules as executable scenarios, for example Gherkin Given/When/Then. Expect tacit knowledge: experts often don't know what they know, so ask questions and listen for terms that get used inconsistently.

See also Evans, "Ubiquitous Language" in the DDD Reference, and Fowler, "UbiquitousLanguage".

3 · Practice

Within one team, people use user, visitor, and account interchangeably for the same concept. Which kind of inconsistency is this?

  1. Synonymous terms
  2. Ambiguous terms
  3. Technical jargon
  4. Missing invariants
Several words for one meaning are synonyms. Ambiguity is the reverse: one word with several meanings. Pick one term, make it explicit, and use it everywhere.

Which sentence belongs in a ubiquitous language?

  1. A campaign can be published only after legal approval.
  2. The campaign row's status column is set to published.
  3. The CampaignService calls the repository and commits the transaction.
  4. A campaign DTO is serialized and sent through Kafka.
Only the first sentence is a business rule in business terms. The others describe implementation: tables, services, DTOs, brokers. A domain expert would never say them, so they don't belong in the language.

In the chapter's sense, a model is…

  1. A simplified representation built to solve a specific problem
  2. A faithful copy of the real world in code
  3. A database schema describing every attribute of the business
  4. A UML diagram produced once before any development begins
A model is useful because of what it leaves out. Trying to copy reality produces bloated models that serve no purpose well. Models evolve with the language. They aren't drawn once up front.

Why does the chapter warn against the chain domain experts → analysts → requirements docs → engineers?

  1. Knowledge degrades at each translation, like a telephone game
  2. Analysts usually lack the technical skills to write code
  3. Documents become outdated faster than code in agile teams
  4. Engineers should never talk to stakeholders without a manager
Each hand-off is a lossy translation, so the code drifts away from what experts actually meant. A shared language lets engineers work with domain experts directly.

A team builds staff scheduling software for a hospital. Who are the domain experts?

  1. The nurses and administrators who schedule shifts every day
  2. The senior engineers who maintain the current scheduling system
  3. The product owner who writes the backlog's user stories
  4. The database administrators who own the hospital scheduling tables
Domain experts are the people who know the business itself, whose knowledge the software has to capture. A product owner can act as a proxy, but relying only on a proxy recreates the telephone game. Engineers and DBAs know the current system, which isn't the same as knowing the domain.

A glossary covers the nouns well. What does the chapter suggest for capturing the domain's behavior and rules?

  1. Gherkin scenarios
  2. UML diagrams
  3. ER diagrams
  4. Javadoc comments
Given/When/Then scenarios state rules in the ubiquitous language, domain experts can read them, and they can run as tests. Entity-relationship (ER) diagrams and comments describe structure or implementation, not behavior.

Where should the ubiquitous language be used?

  1. Conversations, documentation, tests, and the source code itself
  2. Requirements only; code keeps its own technical terms
  3. Meetings with stakeholders only, never inside the codebase
  4. Source code only, so identifiers become the truth
Everywhere. That's what ubiquitous means. If the code uses different names from the conversation, the translation step, and its losses, come back.

4 · Interview scenario

Rentora, a car-rental company. The operations manager explains:

"When a customer books a car we create a reservation. Some people call it a booking. When they collect the car it becomes a rental, and the counter staff call that moment checkout, while the app team calls it pickup. A vehicle and a unit are the same thing for us. If the car comes back late we charge a late fee, unless the delay was our fault, like when we had to swap their car. Oh, and class means the car category (compact, SUV…), but finance uses class for the rental's tax class."

(6 min) (1) List the synonyms to collapse and pick one canonical term for each. (2) Find the ambiguous term and say how you'd resolve it. (3) Write 3 glossary entries. (4) Write one Given/When/Then scenario for the late-fee rule, including its exception.

(1) Synonyms: reservation/booking → Reservation · checkout/pickup → Pickup · vehicle/unit → Vehicle.

(2) Ambiguity: "class" means two different things. In operations, rename it Vehicle category. The tax meaning belongs to finance's model. Strong hint for ch. 3: these are two different bounded contexts, each of which can keep its own meaning.

(3) Glossary (sample): Reservation: a customer's commitment to rent a vehicle category for a period, before pickup. · Rental: the period from pickup to return. · Late fee: a charge for returning after the due time, waived when the company caused the delay.

(4)

Scenario: Late return caused by the customer
  Given a rental due back at 10:00
  When the vehicle is returned at 12:30
  Then a late fee is charged

Scenario: Late return caused by a vehicle swap
  Given a rental due back at 10:00
  And the company swapped the vehicle during the rental
  When the vehicle is returned at 12:30
  Then no late fee is charged
  • Found all three synonym pairs
  • Picked one canonical term for each, in business language
  • Identified "class" as ambiguous and proposed a resolution
  • Glossary entries define what a term is, without technical jargon
  • Wrote the scenario in business terms (no tables, no services)
  • Captured the exception (company-caused delay)
