Chapter 11

Evolving design decisions

Every decision from ch. 10 has an expiry date. Learn to spot when the business has moved under your design.

≈ 12 minutes 2 review + 7 questions + 1 scenario Part III

0 · Spaced review: ch. 1 & 10

  1. Testing pyramid
  2. Reversed pyramid
  3. Testing diamond
  4. Testing trophy
There's little logic to unit test, so weight the suite toward end-to-end tests: a reversed pyramid. Ch. 10.

  2. Supporting subdomain

1 · Recall first

Without looking: (a) Name at least four ways a subdomain can change type, with a plausible trigger for each. (b) When a supporting subdomain becomes core, what should change in its implementation and its integrations? (c) Besides the business domain, what else can change and force design changes?

(a) Core → generic: competitors catch up or a product commoditizes it. Generic → core: the company builds a better-than-market version that becomes an edge. Supporting → core: a simple area turns into a differentiator. Core → supporting: it stops being an edge but stays specific to you. Supporting → generic: an off-the-shelf solution appears. Generic → supporting: you replace a bought product with a simpler in-house version.
(b) Re-run the decision tree. Its logic probably grows, so move it toward a domain model. Revisit the integration patterns as well, for example protecting it with an anticorruption layer instead of conforming to others, and give it your strongest people.
(c) Organization (teams split, move, or grow), domain knowledge (you learn the model was wrong), and growth (subdomains, contexts, or aggregates getting too big).

  • At least four type transitions with triggers
  • Implementation change for supporting → core
  • Integration-pattern change mentioned
  • Organization, knowledge, and growth as other drivers

2 · The ideas that matter

Change is the norm. Design decisions are made with what you know at the time, and the business keeps moving. The chapter looks at four kinds of change: the business domain, the organization, domain knowledge, and growth. Ch. 11.

Subdomain types change. Any transition between core, generic, and supporting is possible. When one happens, the strategic choices (build or buy, who owns it, integration patterns) and the tactical choices (business-logic pattern, architecture, testing) should be revisited using the ch. 10 heuristics.

Tactical evolution. As logic gets more complex, implementations move along the same path as the decision tree: transaction script → active record → domain model → event-sourced domain model. Each step is a refactoring of the existing code, not a rewrite. Moving to event sourcing raises a special question: what about the history you never recorded? You either approximate past events from the current state, or start the stream with a single migration event that represents the current state. Either way, you can't invent history you don't have.

Organizational change. Integration patterns depend on how well teams can communicate. When teams split, grow, or move to different locations, a partnership may need to become customer–supplier, or even separate ways.

Domain knowledge and growth. New insight can show that a boundary or model is wrong, so refactor it. As systems grow, keep distilling: split subdomains that have become too broad, split bounded contexts that hold several models, and shrink aggregates back to what strong consistency actually needs.

See also Fowler, "Event Sourcing".

3 · Practice

Your in-house supporting subdomain for shift rostering is simple. A mature SaaS product now does exactly the same thing. What changed?

  1. Supporting became core
  2. Supporting became generic
  3. Generic became supporting
  4. Core became generic
A ready-made solution now exists, so it's effectively generic. Consider buying it instead of maintaining your own.

A coupon module was a simple supporting subdomain. Marketing now uses its personalized offers to win customers, and its rules keep growing. What should happen to its implementation?

  1. Keep the transaction scripts and add more tests
  2. Replace it immediately with a generic coupon vendor
  3. Re-run the heuristics; evolve toward a domain model
  4. Split it into one microservice per coupon type
Supporting → core: the logic is now complex and it's a differentiator. Re-run the decision tree and refactor gradually toward a domain model. Buying a vendor product would give away the new advantage.

You're migrating an existing state-based aggregate to event sourcing, but past changes were never recorded. Which approach fits the chapter?

  1. Start streams with an event representing current state
  2. Invent detailed history from the product team's memory
  3. Keep both models forever and compare them nightly
  4. Delete old data and start every stream empty
You can't recover history that was never captured. Begin each stream with a migration event that carries the current state, or approximate the past from it. Be explicit that the early history is synthetic.

Two teams in a partnership are split across continents and time zones, and coordination keeps breaking down. Which change fits best?

  1. Merge both bounded contexts into one shared context
  2. Add a shared kernel to coordinate even more
  3. Keep the partnership and schedule more synchronization meetings
  4. Move toward customer–supplier, or perhaps even separate ways
A partnership depends on good communication. When that goes, move to a pattern that needs less coordination: customer–supplier (conformist, ACL, or OHS), or separate ways. A shared kernel needs even more coordination.

An Order aggregate has grown to include shipping history, reviews, and loyalty points, and saving it causes lock contention. What's the remedy?

  1. Add database indexes to speed up every save
  2. Move out data that doesn't need strong consistency
  3. Turn the whole aggregate into its own microservice
  4. Switch the aggregate to active record for simplicity
An aggregate should contain only data that must be strongly consistent with its business rules. Move everything else into other aggregates referenced by ID, and accept eventual consistency between them.

A team bought a generic CRM, but its fixed workflows now limit how the company sells, and selling well is becoming its edge. This transition is…

  1. Generic becoming core
  2. Core becoming supporting
  3. Supporting becoming generic
  4. Generic becoming supporting
An area that was bought off the shelf is turning into a differentiator: generic → core. That justifies building in-house, with a domain model.

Which list contains only drivers of design change that the chapter discusses?

  1. Framework upgrades, cloud migrations, and new hires
  2. Database vendors, programming languages, and IDE choices
  3. Business domain, organization, domain knowledge, and growth
  4. Sprint velocity, code coverage, and deployment frequency
The chapter is about changes in the business domain, organization, domain knowledge, and growth, which are the forces that should drive DDD decisions. The other lists are technical or process concerns.

4 · Interview scenario

CourseCraft is an online-learning marketplace. Three years ago: course catalog (core, a domain model), video hosting (built in-house, a supporting subdomain), and certificates (transaction scripts). Today: video-hosting SaaS products are excellent and cheap. Rivals have copied the catalog. Employers now hire based on CourseCraft's skill-verified certificates, whose rules for proctoring, expiry, and re-validation keep growing. The certificates team was also split in two, across two offices.

(7 min) For each area, state the type transition and what you would change, both strategically and tactically. Then say what you'd do about the certificates team split.

AreaTransitionChange
Video hostingSupporting → genericMigrate to SaaS. Wrap it behind an integration so it can be swapped.
CatalogCore → supporting (or generic)Stop investing heavily in it. Simplify it over time and move the best people elsewhere.
CertificatesSupporting → coreRefactor transaction scripts → domain model. Consider event sourcing for an auditable history of verifications. Protect it with an ACL and expose an OHS.

Team split: give each team its own bounded context, splitting the context along a seam in the language. Integrate them as customer–supplier, not as a cross-office partnership.

  • Video hosting → generic, and buy
  • Catalog demoted from core, with investment reduced
  • Certificates → core, with a domain-model refactoring
  • Considered event sourcing for certificate history (audit)
  • Revisited integration patterns, not only implementation
  • Handled the team split with a pattern that needs less coordination
