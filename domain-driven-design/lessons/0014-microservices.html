Chapter 14

Microservices

A microservice isn't defined by its lines of code. It's defined by how small its public interface is compared to what it hides.

1 · Recall first

Without looking: (a) What makes a service micro, in the chapter's terms? (b) What are global and local complexity, and how does service granularity trade one for the other? (c) Name the widest and the narrowest valid microservice boundaries, and the "safe" boundary in between.

(a) A micro public interface: a deep service, whose simple interface hides a lot of functionality. Size of the codebase is not the measure.
(b) Local complexity is the complexity inside each service. Global complexity is the complexity of the interactions between services. Finer services lower local complexity but raise global complexity. The goal is to balance the two, not to minimize either one.
(c) Widest: bounded contexts. Narrowest: aggregates, usually too narrow. Safe middle: subdomains.

  • Defined micro as a small public interface or deep service
  • Explained the local vs global complexity trade-off
  • Bounded contexts widest, aggregates narrowest
  • Subdomains as the safe heuristic

2 · The ideas that matter

What a service is. A service has a public interface, meaning everything other components can use to talk to it, and an implementation behind it. The chapter warns against the naive reading of "micro" (one method per service, or as few lines of code as possible). That produces swarms of services whose interfaces are as complex as their implementations. Ch. 14.

Deep services. Borrowing John Ousterhout's deep module idea, a good microservice hides a lot of functionality behind a small public interface. A shallow service adds integration cost without hiding anything. Taken to the extreme, you get a distributed big ball of mud.

Global vs local complexity. Breaking a system into finer services reduces each service's local complexity and increases global complexity. Merging services does the reverse. Design so that the total complexity stays manageable.

Boundaries. Every microservice is a bounded context, but not every bounded context is a microservice. A context can be decomposed further. Bounded contexts are the widest valid boundaries. Aggregates are the narrowest, and usually too chatty to be services. Subdomains (sets of coherent use cases) are the safe heuristic, because they tend to be naturally deep.

Compressing public interfaces. Two context-map patterns from ch. 4 reduce what a service exposes. An open-host service publishes a smaller, integration-oriented published language instead of its internal model. An anticorruption layer keeps upstream complexity out of the consumer.

See also Fowler, "BoundedContext" and Ousterhout, A Philosophy of Software Design (deep modules).

3 · Practice

In the chapter's sense, what makes a service micro?

  1. Fewer than a thousand lines of code
  2. Exactly one public method per deployed service
  3. A small public interface hiding rich functionality
  4. A single database table owned by service
Micro refers to the public interface, not the code size. A service with one method per endpoint is the shallow anti-pattern the chapter warns against.

A team splits every business operation into its own service. Each service is trivial, but every feature now needs calls across eight services. What happened?

  1. Local complexity fell while global complexity grew sharply
  2. Global complexity fell while local complexity grew sharply
  3. Both local and global complexity fell together nicely
  4. Neither complexity changed, only the deployment topology did
Each service is simple (low local complexity), but the interactions between them exploded (high global complexity). This road leads to a distributed big ball of mud.

Which statement about microservices and bounded contexts matches the chapter?

  1. Every bounded context must be deployed as one microservice
  2. Microservices and bounded contexts are unrelated design concepts entirely
  3. A microservice should always contain several bounded contexts inside
  4. Every microservice is a bounded context; not vice versa
A microservice has one model and one owner, so it's a bounded context. But a bounded context can be wider than a good microservice and be decomposed further. A classic interview trap is to treat the two as identical.

Why are aggregates usually too narrow to be microservices?

  1. Aggregates cannot be persisted in separate databases at all
  2. Use cases span aggregates, making services shallow and chatty
  3. Aggregates are only ever valid inside event-sourced domain models
  4. Aggregates must always share one transaction with their neighbors
Business processes usually touch several aggregates, so one service per aggregate means lots of cross-service traffic and shallow interfaces. Subdomains group coherent use cases, so they tend to give deeper services.

Which boundary does the chapter offer as a safe heuristic for microservices?

  1. Align with subdomains
  2. Align with aggregates
  3. Align with entities
  4. Align with tables
Subdomains are coherent sets of use cases, so they're naturally deep. Aggregates are too narrow. Entities and tables are technical, not business, boundaries.

A service exposes its entire internal model to twelve consumers. Every internal refactor breaks someone. Which ch. 4 pattern compresses its public interface?

  1. Shared kernel
  2. Conformist relationship
  3. Open-host service
  4. Partnership agreement
An open-host service publishes a separate, integration-oriented published language. The interface shrinks, and the internal model can change without breaking consumers. A shared kernel would widen the coupling instead.

What does the chapter call the failure mode of naive, very fine-grained microservices?

  1. An anemic domain model everywhere
  2. Distributed big ball of mud
  3. Integration through a shared database
  4. A single event-sourced modular monolith
The distributed big ball of mud has all the tangles of a monolith, plus network latency and partial failures.

4 · Interview scenario

Tutorly, an online tutoring marketplace. Its edge is tutor–student matching, which uses learning-style assessments. It also handles session scheduling, video calls (through a third-party SDK), payments and payouts (through a payment provider), and reviews and ratings. The current architecture plan has 23 microservices: one per entity (Tutor, Student, Session, Review, Payout, …). The CTO asks you: "Is this a good decomposition? What would you propose instead?"

(7 min) Critique the 23-service plan using the chapter's vocabulary. Then propose service boundaries, justify them, and name one way to keep each service's public interface small.

Critique: one service per entity gives shallow services. Every use case (booking a session, paying a tutor) spans several of them, so local complexity is low and global complexity explodes. That's heading for a distributed big ball of mud.

Proposal (align with subdomains):

ServiceSubdomainType
MatchingTutor–student matching, assessmentsCore
SchedulingSessions and availabilitySupporting
BillingPayments and payouts (wraps the provider)Generic + supporting
ReviewsRatings and reviewsSupporting
VideoThin adapter over the SDK, or no service at allGeneric

Compression: Matching acts as an open-host service and publishes, for example, MatchProposed in a published language. Billing puts an anticorruption layer in front of the payment provider. If Matching turns out to be too big, split it later, along its own subdomains, not along entities.

  • Named the entity-per-service plan as shallow services
  • Explained the local vs global complexity trade-off
  • Proposed boundaries aligned with subdomains
  • Did not put one aggregate or entity per service
  • Used OHS/published language or an ACL to compress interfaces
  • Noted that every service is a bounded context, and that a context could be split further later
