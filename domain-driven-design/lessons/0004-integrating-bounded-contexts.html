Chapter 4

Integrating bounded contexts

Contexts are separate, but they're never isolated. The integration pattern you choose reflects how the teams relate to each other.

2 · The ideas that matter

Contracts. Wherever two bounded contexts integrate, there's a contract. Each side has its own model and language, so someone has to translate. The patterns differ in who does the translating, and that depends on how the teams work together. Ch. 4.

Cooperation works when teams communicate well.

  • Partnership: the teams coordinate integration ad hoc and in both directions, and they fix breakages together.
  • Shared kernel: part of the model is shared and co-owned, so a change affects both contexts. It's justified when duplicating that part would cost more than coordinating changes to it. Keep the shared part small.

Customer–supplier: the upstream (supplier) can succeed without the downstream (customer). The balance of power decides which pattern fits.

  • Conformist: the downstream accepts the upstream model as it is. This is reasonable when the upstream model is an industry standard or simply good enough.
  • Anticorruption layer: the downstream translates the upstream model into its own. Use it when the downstream is core, when the upstream model is inefficient or inconvenient, or when the upstream contract changes often.
  • Open-host service: the upstream exposes a published language that is separate from its internal model, so it can change internally without breaking consumers. It can serve several versions of that language at once.

Separate ways: when collaboration is too hard, or duplication is cheap (as with generic functionality), each side duplicates what it needs. Avoid it for core subdomains, because duplicating your competitive advantage contradicts your strategy.

Context map: a diagram of the contexts and the patterns between them. It shows the high-level design, communication patterns, and organizational problems. For example, if every consumer of one upstream team builds an anticorruption layer, something is wrong upstream.

See also DDD Crew, context mapping cheat sheet and Evans's DDD Reference (Context Mapping section).

3 · Practice

Two teams sit in the same office. They coordinate integration changes as needed, either team can break the other, and when that happens they fix it together. Which pattern is this?

  1. Partnership pattern
  2. Shared kernel
  3. Conformist relationship
  4. Separate ways
Partnership means two-way, ad hoc coordination between teams that succeed or fail together. Nothing here says they share model code, so it isn't a shared kernel.

When is a shared kernel justified?

  1. When two teams cannot communicate well with each other
  2. When the upstream model is messy and changes often
  3. When duplicating the model costs more than coordinating changes
  4. When one context must publish data to many consumers
A shared kernel couples the two contexts, so it's worth it only when duplication would cost more than coordination. If teams can't communicate, choose separate ways or an anticorruption layer. A messy upstream calls for an anticorruption layer, and many consumers call for an open-host service.

Your downstream context consumes an upstream API that uses a well-known industry-standard data format. The format fits your needs, and the upstream team won't customize it for you. Which pattern fits best?

  1. Anticorruption layer
  2. Conformist relationship
  3. Shared kernel
  4. Partnership pattern
The upstream model is a standard and good enough, so translating it buys you nothing. Accept it: conformist. You'd only pay for an anticorruption layer if the upstream model would hurt your own.

Which of these is not a typical reason to build an anticorruption layer?

  1. Your downstream context implements a core business subdomain
  2. The upstream model is inconvenient for your needs
  3. The upstream contract changes frequently and without warning
  4. The upstream model already fits your needs perfectly
If the upstream model already fits, a translation layer is pure cost. Conform instead. The other three are the chapter's reasons to protect your model with an anticorruption layer.

An upstream Catalog context has a dozen consumers. Its team wants to refactor its internal model often without breaking any of them. Which pattern should Catalog adopt?

  1. Anticorruption layer
  2. Open-host service
  3. Shared kernel
  4. Conformist relationship
An open-host service exposes a published language that is separate from the internal model. The upstream absorbs the translation, so its internals can change freely, and it can even serve several versions of the public language. An anticorruption layer is something a downstream team builds.

When does the chapter say to avoid separate ways?

  1. When duplicating generic functionality
  2. When teams cannot collaborate
  3. When integrating core subdomains
  4. When models differ greatly
Duplicating a core subdomain means building your competitive advantage twice, which contradicts the strategy. The other three are the usual reasons to choose separate ways.

A context map shows five downstream contexts, and every one of them wraps the same upstream context in an anticorruption layer. What might this signal?

  1. The upstream model is serving its consumers poorly
  2. The downstream teams are all over-engineering their integrations
  3. The organization should merge all six contexts together
  4. The upstream team should adopt a conformist relationship
A context map is also an organizational diagnostic. When everyone downstream protects themselves from one upstream, the upstream model or interface is the problem. An open-host service is the usual fix.

4 · Interview scenario

PawPoint, continued (from ch. 3). The contexts are Scheduling (core, Scheduling team), Clinical (Clinical team), Billing and Inventory (both owned by the Billing team), plus two external systems:

  • A third-party lab sends test results in an old, awkward format that changes without notice.
  • A payment provider offers a widely used, well-designed API.

Billing needs to know which treatments were performed. Scheduling needs typical visit durations from clinical history, and the two teams work closely together. Billing and Inventory share a medicine price list.

(7 min) Sketch the context map. For each relationship, name the upstream and downstream (if there is one), the pattern, and a one-line justification.

RelationshipPatternWhy
Lab (up) → Clinical (down)Anticorruption layerThe upstream is messy and volatile and won't change for us. Protect the model.
Payment provider (up) → Billing (down)ConformistA generic subdomain with a good, widely used model. Translating adds nothing.
Clinical (up) → Billing (down)Open-host service (published language, e.g. a "treatment performed" contract)Clinical's internals can evolve, and other consumers (e.g. Scheduling) can reuse the same contract.
Scheduling ↔ ClinicalPartnershipThe teams work closely together and succeed together. Two-way coordination is cheap.
Billing ↔ InventoryShared kernel (price list)One team owns both, so coordination is cheap and duplication would cost more.

Defensible variations: Clinical could conform to the lab if its model were good enough, and Billing could build an anticorruption layer over Clinical instead of Clinical offering an open-host service. In an interview, the justification is what gets scored.

  • Anticorruption layer for the lab, justified by a messy or volatile upstream
  • Conformist (or a justified alternative) for the payment provider
  • Open-host service or published language for Clinical → Billing, or a justified anticorruption layer downstream
  • Partnership for Scheduling ↔ Clinical, justified by the close collaboration
  • Shared kernel for Billing ↔ Inventory, justified by coordination vs duplication cost
  • Marked upstream and downstream for every customer–supplier relationship
