Chapter 1

Analyzing business domains

Every later decision in the book starts from one question: what kind of subdomain is this?

≈ 12 minutes 1 recall + 7 questions + 1 scenario Part I

Why this chapter matters in an interview: "build or buy?", "where do your best engineers go?", and "how much architecture does this need?" all come down to classifying subdomains. Get the classification wrong and every downstream choice is wrong with it. Chapter 10's decision tree starts from this answer.

2 · The ideas that matter

Business domain: the company's main area of activity. Subdomains: the finer-grained areas of activity that together make it work. You discover subdomains by analyzing the business. You don't design them. Ch. 1.

  • Core is what the company does differently from its competitors. It doesn't have to be technical: if a company's edge is how it designs its products, the software around that may not be core at all. A core that is simple to build gives only a short-lived advantage, because competitors copy it.
  • Generic is complex, but every company does it the same way, so it's solved. Authentication, payments, and accounting are typical. Buy or adopt.
  • Supporting is needed but simple and not a differentiator, often CRUD or ETL. Build it cheaply or outsource it.

Finding the boundaries: start coarse, with the company's departments and organizational units. Then distill: one coarse subdomain can hide finer subdomains of different types. Stop when each subdomain is a set of coherent use cases (closely related use cases, typically involving the same actors and the same data). Distilling pays off most in the core, where the details decide where you invest.

Evans's original framing of the same idea: the "Core Domain" and "Generic Subdomains" in the DDD Reference (Distillation section).

3 · Practice

What separates a generic subdomain from a supporting one?

  1. Generic is complex but solved; supporting is simple but specific
  2. Generic is simple but specific; supporting is complex but solved
  3. Generic gives a competitive advantage; supporting never gives any advantage
  4. Generic is always outsourced; supporting is always built entirely in-house
Neither one gives a competitive advantage. The difference is complexity. Generic subdomains are hard (think of writing your own payment processing), but they're solved, so you buy. Supporting subdomains are easy and specific to your company, so you build them cheaply. Supporting can be outsourced, but it doesn't have to be.

A small furniture maker wins customers through its distinctive hand-built designs. It sells online through an off-the-shelf hosted e-commerce platform. The online store is a…

  1. Core subdomain
  2. Generic subdomain
  3. Supporting subdomain
  4. Bounded context
Generic: running a web shop is complex but solved, and the maker buys it. The core subdomain here is furniture design, which isn't software at all. Core doesn't mean technical.

A startup wants to outsource its core subdomain to save money. What's the strongest objection the chapter supports?

  1. Its constant change demands tight, in-house domain knowledge
  2. Outsourced code is always lower quality than in-house
  3. Core subdomains are too simple to justify contracts
  4. Generic vendors legally cannot implement core business logic
Core subdomains are complex and volatile, and they're where the competitive advantage lives. Constant change needs domain knowledge close at hand, and handing the differentiator to outsiders is a strategic risk. Hence: in-house, with your best people.

A founder says: "Our core subdomain is a simple form-and-table app. A competitor could build it in a week." What does the chapter imply?

  1. A simple core gives only a short-lived advantage
  2. Simple core subdomains are best implemented by vendors
  3. Complexity is irrelevant; only its revenue really counts
  4. Simple subdomains are automatically generic, whatever the strategy
An advantage that's easy to copy won't last. Core subdomains are typically complex because they're hard to imitate. Generic means complex and solved, so simplicity doesn't make something generic.

Where does the chapter suggest you start when identifying a company's subdomains?

  1. The company's departments and other organizational units
  2. The existing database schema and its tables
  3. The microservices the engineering team already runs
  4. The vendors the company already pays for
Start coarse, from the organization (sales, marketing, customer service…), then distill. Database tables and running services are design artifacts. Subdomains come from the business.

When should you stop breaking subdomains into finer ones?

  1. When each is a set of coherent use cases
  2. When each maps to exactly one single database table
  3. When each can be owned by one single developer
  4. When each is small enough to be a microservice
Subdomains are sets of coherent use cases: closely related, typically sharing actors and data. Going finer adds no strategic information. The other three options are technical or organizational criteria, not business ones.

At Hopwise, "Customer Support" looks like a supporting subdomain at the department level. But inside it is an in-house fraud-detection model that rivals can't match. Fraud detection is a…

  1. Core subdomain
  2. Generic subdomain
  3. Supporting subdomain
  4. Bounded context
This is why you distill: a coarse subdomain can contain finer subdomains of different types. Fraud detection gives a real competitive edge, so it's core, even though the department around it isn't.

4 · Interview scenario

Brewly sells coffee subscriptions. Its edge is personalized roasts: it adjusts each customer's roast profile from their taste feedback, and rivals can't match the results. Departments: Roasting, Subscriptions & Billing, Shipping (labels through carrier APIs, plus letting customers pick delivery days), Customer Support (uses a ticketing tool), and Marketing (promo codes and referral credits).

(6 min) Start from the departments, then distill. List Brewly's subdomains, give each a type, and justify it in one line. Split at least one department into finer subdomains of different types.

DepartmentSubdomainType
RoastingTaste-profile personalizationCore: the stated edge
Roast batch schedulingSupporting: needed, simple
Subscriptions & BillingSubscription plans & pausesSupporting
Card paymentsGeneric: buy
ShippingLabel generation via carriersGeneric: buy or adopt
Delivery-day selectionSupporting
Customer SupportTicketingGeneric: off-the-shelf tool
MarketingPromo codes & referralsSupporting (core only if it were a real differentiator)

Defensible variations are fine. In an interview, the justification is what gets scored.

  • Started from the departments, then distilled
  • Taste personalization as core, justified by competitive edge
  • Payments, shipping labels, and ticketing as generic (buy)
  • Plans, delivery days, and promos as supporting
  • Split at least one department into subdomains of different types
  • Stopped at coherent use cases, not at technical components
